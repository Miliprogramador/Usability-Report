Abstract

This report assesses the usability of UDLAP Smart Parking with eleven questionnaire responses, covering the student’s semester and thirteen application-specific questions. The purpose of involving students from different semesters was to obtain a wider range of perspectives: some students may still be learning the layout of campus, while others may have established routines. Semester itself was not measured as a direct indicator of experience, and it is not the main focus of the evaluation.

Findings indicate high perceived usability: 11/11 gave navigation the maximum rating, 10/11 gave overall satisfaction the maximum rating, and 11/11 said they could identify available parking. However, map interpretation varied, and three respondents reported at least one concern. Each question is documented using a graph, counts, percentages, an explanation, and a design implication. The report includes prioritized recommendations and an evidence-based design-iteration plan. Results come from a self-report survey, so no objective task completion, timings, SUS score, or verified occupancy accuracy is claimed.

Introduction

The Smart Parking App aims to help the UDLAP community locate parking areas close to their destination while making the availability of spaces understandable from a smartphone. The preceding Technical Design Report described the problem, personas, prototype design, and proposed testing tasks. The later supplied application video shows named academic destinations, availability indicators, parking registration, and a release action. This evaluation considers students’ reported experience with the app, and uses the results to guide the next iteration.

The central questions are whether the app is easy to understand, whether parking information and the map are clear, whether the mobile interface feels comfortable, and which problems should be fixed before broader deployment.

Testing Methodology

Participants and the reason for different semesters

The response spreadsheet contains 11 questionnaires: three first-semester students, two third-semester students, five students in fourth semester or above, and one respondent who selected conflicting semester categories. Involving different semesters is useful because the app should work for people with potentially different campus familiarity, not just those who already know the parking areas. We do not assume that being in an early semester necessarily means lacking campus experience; no question directly measured knowledge of UDLAP’s layout. The inconsistent semester entry is shown separately in the overall data and excluded from cohort comparisons only.

Tasks, environment, and protocol

The previous Technical Design Report proposed five observed scenarios: (T1) identify parking near a building, (T2) select an appropriate area, (T3) register parking, (T4) release a space, and (T5) correct a wrong selection. The completed questionnaire instead measures perceived ease, clarity, navigation, map comprehension, availability recognition, visual design, comfort, satisfaction, and intended use. The data export does not contain observed task outcomes, response times, assistance, or a record of the setting, device, or test moderator for each respondent. Therefore, this report does not claim that a controlled task-based test was completed.

Metrics and analysis rules

All charts use N = 11; category percentages equal 100 × count/11. Q1, Q5, Q8, and Q12 are five-point ratings, for which means are reported. Q4, Q9, and Q11 allow multiple selections, so their percentages can add to more than 100%. The analysis includes only the semester question and Q1–Q13, as requested; the last two free-text survey questions are not included. Thus, no open-ended user quotes or click heatmaps can be responsibly reported from the analyzed columns.

Limitations and participant privacy

The sample is small and nonrandom, and results are descriptive rather than generalizable to all UDLAP students. Names and timestamps are omitted from reporting. The earlier testing plan targeted eight to ten people, while the actual export contains eleven respondents. These are survey responses; objective success rates, stopwatch timings, error logs, accessibility conformance, and SUS results require a separate study.

Data Analysis

Major usability concerns

Accuracy and trust of parking status. All eleven respondents could recognize available parking areas in Q7, but one respondent in Q11 selected the option that information seemed incorrect. Recognition of a status is not evidence that the reported space is truly free. Because the previous design relies on manual reports, information freshness deserves high priority even though the issue was only reported once.

Reliability and interruptions. One respondent selected both slow screen/map loading and crashes in Q11. This is one person’s report, not two separate affected users. If reproduced, it could interrupt the register/release workflow and should be fixed before wider use.

Map orientation. Only 7/11 participants described map interpretation as very easy; 3/11 selected somewhat easy and 1/11 neutral. One person additionally indicated trouble identifying their current position. These signals justify testing landmarks and location cues.

Minor issues and positive findings

Three people selected icons as difficult to understand, two selected buttons, and one selected texts/instructions (Q4); selections overlap and only three respondents flagged any element. More positive patterns include Q1 (10/11 rated initial ease 5/5), Q5 (11/11 rated navigation 5/5), Q8 (10/11 rated organization 5/5), Q10 (11/11 very comfortable on mobile), and Q13 (10/11 definitely willing to use the app). These outcomes favor targeted fixes rather than a complete redesign.

Campus familiarity: an exploratory comparison

The purpose of using several semesters was to gather perspectives from students who may have different levels of knowledge of the university. The app should be straightforward even if a user does not yet know the parking layout. The comparison below is illustrative, not the central outcome of this report.

In particular, the problems mentioned by first-semester respondents concerned information reliability and performance, which cannot automatically be attributed to unfamiliarity with the campus. A future questionnaire should directly ask how frequently respondents park and how well they know campus locations.

Unexpected behaviors and accessibility

A useful apparent contradiction is that everyone could recognize parking availability (Q7), while one person questioned its accuracy (Q11). Similarly, everyone rated navigation at the maximum (Q5), although some selected specific confusing symbols (Q4). Usability, trust, and reliability are related but separate concepts. Accessibility was not tested with screen readers, text enlargement, high-glare conditions, or motor-accessibility tools; those tests remain necessary. No original free-text quotations or gaze/click heatmaps are available in the selected questionnaire fields.

Actionable Recommendations

The severity values use the Nielsen-style 0–4 scale as team estimates of potential consequences. They are not independently measured failure rates. P1 means next iteration; P2 means improvement after high-priority issues.

R1 — Communicate freshness of parking information

Problem: A parking count may be outdated or perceived as incorrect. Evidence: one Q11 report, plus manual-update limitations described in the Technical Design Report. Severity: 3/4, potentially major if a driver is directed to a full lot. Solution: display “Last updated”, indicate uncertain/stale status, and allow correction of wrong availability. Expected impact: better calibrated trust and fewer incorrect decisions. Priority: P1. Validation: test decisions made with fresh versus stale status.

R2 — Improve map orientation

Problem: Map interpretation is less uniformly easy than navigation. Evidence: Q6 has four responses other than “very easy”; Q11 includes one location difficulty. Severity: 2/4. Solution: labeled landmarks, map legend, optional “You are here” marker or manual starting point. Expected impact: quicker identification of appropriate parking areas. Priority: P1. Validation: timed, unassisted map tasks involving people of varied familiarity, not just varied semesters.

R3 — Diagnose and handle performance problems

Problem: App delays or crashes can disrupt parking registration. Evidence: one respondent marked slow loading and unexpected errors in Q11. Severity: potentially 3/4 if reproducible. Solution: reproduce under weak connectivity, add loading/retry states, protect against duplicate updates. Expected impact: more reliable completion and clearer recovery. Priority: P1 investigation. Validation: measure repeatable failures and successful retries.

R4 — Clarify icons and buttons

Problem: Individual controls can be unclear even when the overall flow is simple. Evidence: Q4 icons (3/11), buttons (2/11), texts (1/11); selections overlap. Severity: 2/4. Solution: pair important icons with plain labels and standardize primary actions. Expected impact: reduced confusion without unnecessary screens. Priority: P2. Validation: control-recognition and error-recovery exercises.

R5 — Run an objective follow-up test

Problem: Survey ratings do not measure task completion or accessibility. Evidence: no recorded T1–T5 performance, timing, error count, SUS responses, or accessibility audit. Severity: 2/4 as an evaluation limitation. Solution: moderated tasks with both campus newcomers and experienced users, plus SUS and accessibility checks. Expected impact: stronger evidence for design decisions. Priority: P1. Validation: compare the measured performance with the predeclared goals in the technical plan (at least 80% unassisted completion per task and SUS at least 68).

Design Iterations

Documented design evolution: previous and current states

The October Technical Design Report described a Canva-linked interface with four lettered buildings, example availability values, and a planned parking registration flow. The later supplied smartphone video shows named academic destination categories, vacancy badges, occupied/full states, confirmation feedback, a parking timer, and an explicit “Ya me fui” release control. This demonstrates visual and interaction development, but the survey does not establish why the earlier changes were implemented or that backend availability is accurate.

Survey-driven changes proposed for the next iteration

The next version should add availability timestamps (Q11), clearer campus-orientation cues (Q6/Q11), error and loading recovery (Q11), and improved icon labeling (Q4). These are recommended future modifications, not changes already verified. Before/after evaluation will use comparable T1–T5 scenarios, measured time, error and assistance counts, and post-task ratings. The team should recruit students with both low and high measured campus familiarity to test whether the revised app is understandable to everyone.

Conclusion

Across eleven responses, UDLAP Smart Parking received excellent self-reported results for first-use ease, navigation, information clarity, mobile comfort, overall satisfaction, and interest in future use. Nevertheless, the data identifies realistic opportunities for improvements in map comprehension, symbol clarity, information reliability, and app stability. Involving students from different semesters helps broaden the perspectives collected, but no single semester defines the app’s success or failure. The practical design objective is a clear, dependable parking experience for anyone who needs it at UDLAP.

The present report documents attitudes and reported difficulties, not direct task success, real-time vacancy accuracy, or changes in actual time spent finding parking. The proposed next step is to implement a small set of targeted changes and verify them in a controlled, accessible usability test.
